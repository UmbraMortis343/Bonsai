graph: Measurement
Measurement | Putting numbers and units on physical quantities
  Length and volume | Measured with rulers and measuring cylinders
    Measuring multiples | Measure many small lengths together, such as a stack of sheets, and divide
    Volume by displacement | An object's volume is the water it pushes aside
  Time intervals | Measured with clocks and timers
    ! Timing many swings | Time several oscillations and divide to cut the error in one
  Scalars and vectors | A scalar has size only, a vector has size and direction
    ! Adding two vectors at right angles | Draw them head to tail and find the resultant by Pythagoras or scale drawing
  Averaging readings | Repeating and averaging reduces random error

graph: Motion
Motion | How position changes with time
  Speed | Distance travelled per unit time
    Average speed | Total distance divided by total time
    Velocity | Speed in a stated direction
  Acceleration | Change of velocity per unit time
    Deceleration | A negative acceleration: slowing down
    ! Using a = Δv / t | Find acceleration from a change in velocity and the time taken
  Distance–time graphs | The gradient gives the speed
  Speed–time graphs | The gradient gives acceleration and the area gives distance
    ! Distance from a speed–time graph | Add the areas under the line
  Free fall | Falling under gravity alone, about 9.8 m/s² near Earth
    Terminal velocity | Constant speed reached when air resistance balances weight

graph: Forces
Forces | Pushes and pulls that change motion or shape
  Mass and weight | Mass is the amount of matter, weight the pull of gravity on it
    Gravitational field strength | Force per unit mass, about 9.8 N/kg on Earth
    ! Using W = mg | Weight is mass times gravitational field strength
  Resultant force | The single force equal to all the forces acting together
    Motion in a circle | A force at right angles to the motion keeps it on a circle; more speed or mass needs more force, more force tightens the circle
    Balanced forces | Zero resultant: at rest or at constant velocity
    Force and acceleration | A resultant force makes a mass accelerate: F = ma
    ! Using F = ma | Relate resultant force, mass and acceleration
  Friction | A force opposing motion between surfaces or through a fluid
  Stretching springs | Extension is proportional to load up to a limit
    Spring constant | Force per unit extension
    Limit of proportionality | Beyond it, extension is no longer proportional to load
  Moments | The turning effect of a force: force times perpendicular distance from the pivot
    Principle of moments | In equilibrium, clockwise moments equal anticlockwise moments
    Centre of gravity | The point where all the weight seems to act
      Stability | A low centre of gravity and a wide base make an object harder to tip over
      ! Finding the centre of gravity | Hang a lamina from two points and mark where the plumb lines cross
    ! Balancing a beam | Use the principle of moments to find an unknown force or distance
  Momentum | Mass times velocity
    ! Using F = Δp / Δt | Resultant force is the change in momentum per unit time
    Impulse | Force times time, equal to the change in momentum
    Conservation of momentum | Total momentum is unchanged when no external force acts
    ! Solving a collision | Equate total momentum before and after

graph: Energy
Energy | The capacity to do work, moved between stores
  Energy stores | Kinetic, gravitational, chemical, elastic, nuclear, internal, electrostatic
    Energy transfers | Moved between stores by forces, electric currents, heating and waves
    Kinetic energy | Energy of motion: ½mv²
    Gravitational potential energy | Energy of height: mgh
  Conservation of energy | Energy is never made or destroyed, only transferred
    Sankey diagrams | Arrow widths show where the energy goes
    ! Falling object energy | Set the loss in mgh equal to the gain in ½mv²
  Work | Force times distance moved in the direction of the force
    ! Using W = Fd | Work done equals energy transferred
  Power | Work done, or energy transferred, per unit time
  Efficiency | Useful output over total input
    ! Calculating efficiency | Useful energy or power out divided by total in, as a percentage
  Energy resources | Sources we draw energy from
    Power stations | A boiler, turbine and generator turn a fuel's energy into electricity
    Comparing energy resources | Renewability, availability, reliability, scale and environmental impact
    The Sun as the source | Its radiation is behind every resource except geothermal, nuclear and tidal
    Fusion power research | Work to make electricity from fusion on a large scale
    Renewable resources | Replaced as they are used: solar, wind, hydro, tidal, geothermal
      Solar cells and solar panels | Cells turn light into electricity; panels use infrared to heat water
      Biofuels | Fuels from plants, a renewable store of chemical energy
    Fossil fuels | Coal, oil and gas: a store of chemical energy that runs out and emits CO₂
    Nuclear fuel | Energy released by fission in a reactor

graph: Density and pressure
Density | Mass per unit volume
  ! Measuring density | Mass on a balance; volume by measuring, or by displacement for an irregular solid
  ! Using ρ = m / V | Find density from measured mass and volume
  Floating and sinking | An object floats in a liquid of greater density
    Liquids that float on each other | The less dense liquid floats on top, if they do not mix
  Pressure | Force per unit area
    ! Using p = F / A | The same force on a smaller area gives a larger pressure
    Pressure in liquids | Increases with depth and with the liquid's density
    ! Using Δp = ρgΔh | Pressure change with depth in a liquid

graph: Thermal physics
Thermal physics | Heat, temperature and the particles behind them
  Kinetic particle model | Matter is made of moving particles
    Kelvin scale | T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273
    Gas pressure and temperature | At fixed volume a hotter gas's particles hit harder and more often, so pressure rises
    Gas pressure and volume | At fixed temperature a smaller volume means more collisions per second, so pressure rises
    Particles in each state | Close and fixed in solids, close but moving in liquids, far apart and fast in gases
    States of matter | Solids hold shape, liquids flow, gases fill their container
    Brownian motion | Random jiggling of visible specks, evidence for moving molecules
    Gas pressure | Caused by particles colliding with the walls
    Absolute zero | The lowest temperature, −273 °C, where particles have least energy
    ! Boyle's law at constant temperature | pV stays constant for a fixed mass of gas
  Thermal expansion | Most materials expand when heated
    Expansion of solids, liquids and gases | Gases expand most and solids least, because of how tightly the particles are held
    Uses of thermal expansion | Liquid thermometers, bimetallic strips, gaps in rails and bridges
  Specific heat capacity | Energy needed to raise 1 kg by 1 °C
    Temperature and particle energy | A temperature rise is a rise in the particles' average kinetic energy, and so in internal energy
    ! Measuring specific heat capacity | Heat a known mass with a known energy and record the temperature rise
    ! Using ΔE = mcΔθ | Energy to change the temperature of a mass
  Changes of state | Melting, boiling, condensing, freezing at fixed temperatures
    Melting and boiling points of water | 0 °C and 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure
    Condensation and solidification | Particles lose energy and come closer together
    Evaporation | Faster particles escape from a liquid's surface, cooling it
      Factors affecting evaporation | Faster with a higher temperature, a larger surface area and moving air
      Boiling and evaporation compared | Boiling happens at one temperature throughout the liquid; evaporation at any temperature, from the surface
    Boiling | Bubbles form throughout the liquid at one temperature
  Thermal energy transfer | Energy moves from hot to cold
    Thermal transfer in everyday life | Pans, heating a room by convection, a wood fire, a car radiator
    Conduction | Transfer through a material by particle vibration and free electrons
      Poor conduction in fluids | Particles in gases and most liquids are too far apart to pass vibrations on well
    Convection | Transfer by a fluid moving as density changes
    Thermal radiation | Transfer by infrared waves; needs no medium
      Radiation balance | A body stays at a constant temperature when it gives out energy as fast as it takes it in
      Earth's temperature | Set by the balance between incoming radiation and radiation from the surface
      Rate of emission | Rises with surface temperature and surface area
    Good absorbers and emitters | Dull black surfaces absorb and emit best, shiny ones worst

graph: Waves
Waves | Transfers of energy without transfer of matter
  Wave features | Wavelength, frequency, amplitude, crest, trough, speed
    Frequency | Number of waves passing a point each second
    Wavelength | Distance between matching points on neighbouring waves
    ! Using v = fλ | Wave speed is frequency times wavelength
  Transverse waves | Vibration at right angles to the direction of travel
  Longitudinal waves | Vibration along the direction of travel
    Seismic waves | S-waves are transverse, P-waves longitudinal
  Reflection of waves | Bouncing off a surface
  Refraction of waves | Change of speed, and often direction, in a new medium
  Diffraction | Spreading through a gap or round an edge
    Gap size and wavelength | Most spreading when the gap is about the size of the wavelength
    Ripple tank | Shows water waves reflecting, refracting and diffracting
  Sound | A longitudinal wave made by vibrating sources
    Compressions and rarefactions | Regions of higher and lower pressure in a sound wave
    Range of human hearing | About 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz
    Speed of sound | About 330–350 m/s in air; faster in liquids and faster still in solids
    Sound needs a medium | It cannot travel through a vacuum
    Pitch and loudness | Higher frequency is higher pitch, larger amplitude is louder
    Echoes | Reflected sound
    Ultrasound | Sound above 20 kHz, used for scans and sonar
    ! Speed of sound from an echo | Distance there and back divided by the time

graph: Light
Light | A transverse wave we can see
  Law of reflection | Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection
    Plane mirror image | Virtual, upright, same size, as far behind as the object is in front
  Refraction of light | Light bends when it changes speed between materials
    Refractive index | The ratio of the speeds of light in two materials; from air, n = sin i / sin r
    ! Using n = sin i / sin r | Find the refractive index or an angle
    Critical angle | The angle of incidence beyond which light cannot leave the denser medium
      ! Using n = 1 / sin c | Critical angle from the refractive index
    Total internal reflection | All the light reflects inside the denser medium
    Optical fibres | Carry light by repeated total internal reflection
  Thin lenses | Shaped glass that converges or diverges light
    Principal focus | Where rays parallel to the principal axis meet after a converging lens
    Magnifying glass | An object inside the focal length of a converging lens gives an enlarged, upright, virtual image
    Correcting sight | Converging lenses for long sight, diverging lenses for short sight
    Focal length | Distance from the lens to the principal focus
    Real and virtual images | A real image forms on a screen, a virtual one cannot
    ! Drawing a ray diagram | Two rays from the top of the object locate the image
  Dispersion | A prism splits white light into colours
    Colours of the spectrum | Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet, in order of rising frequency
    Monochromatic light | Light of a single frequency
  Electromagnetic spectrum | Radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma
    Communication with EM waves | Microwaves for mobiles, Wi-Fi and satellites; radio waves for Bluetooth; light and infrared in optical fibres
    Digital and analogue signals | Digital takes only set values, analogue varies continuously; sound can be sent either way
    Benefits of digital signals | Faster data, and longer range because the signal can be regenerated exactly
    Speed of electromagnetic waves | All travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum
    Uses and dangers of EM waves | From radio links to medical imaging; higher frequencies damage cells

graph: Electricity
Electricity | Charge and its flow round circuits
  Electric charge | Positive and negative; like charges repel, unlike attract
    Charging by friction | Electrons move from one insulator to another
    Electric field | A region where a charge feels a force
      Electric field patterns | Radial round a point charge or charged sphere, uniform between parallel plates; the direction is the force on a positive charge
    Conductors and insulators | Conductors have free electrons, insulators do not
  Current | Rate of flow of charge
    Direct and alternating current | d.c. flows one way, a.c. keeps reversing
    Ammeters and voltmeters | Ammeters go in series, voltmeters in parallel
    ! Using I = Q / t | Current is charge passing per unit time
    Conventional current | Flows from positive to negative, opposite to the electrons
  Potential difference | Work done per unit charge between two points
    Electromotive force | Work done per unit charge by a source driving charge round a circuit
  Resistance | Potential difference divided by current
    ! Using V = IR | Relate voltage, current and resistance
    Resistance of a wire | Grows with length, falls with cross-sectional area
    Current–voltage graphs | A straight line for a resistor, a curve for a lamp, one-way for a diode
  Electrical power | Energy transferred per second: P = IV
    Electrical energy | E = IVt
    Kilowatt-hour | The energy used by 1 kW for one hour
    ! Cost of electricity | Energy in kWh times the price per unit
  Series circuits | One path: the same current everywhere, voltages add
    Cells in series | Their e.m.f.s add
  Parallel circuits | Several paths: the same voltage across each, currents add
    Lamps in parallel | Each gets the full voltage and can be switched on its own
    ! Combined resistance | Add resistors in series; in parallel the total is less than the smallest
  Potential divider | Two resistors share a voltage in proportion to their resistance
    ! Using R₁ / R₂ = V₁ / V₂ | Each resistor's share of the voltage
    Diodes and LEDs | Let current through one way only; an LED gives out light
    Thermistors and LDRs | Resistance falls with temperature, or with light
  Electrical safety | Protecting people and wiring from too much current
    Mains wiring | Live, neutral and earth wires; the switch and fuse go in the live wire
    Double insulation | A non-conducting case needs no earth wire
    Mains hazards | Damaged insulation, overheating cables, damp, overloaded sockets
    ! Choosing a fuse | Pick the rating just above the normal current
    Fuses and circuit breakers | Break the circuit when the current is too large
    Earthing | A low-resistance path to ground for metal cases

graph: Magnetism and electromagnetism
Magnetism | Forces between magnets and magnetic materials
  Magnetic poles | North and south; like poles repel, unlike attract
    Magnetic and non-magnetic materials | Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are magnetic; most other materials are not
    Induced magnetism | A magnetic material becomes a magnet while near one
    Hard and soft magnetic materials | Steel keeps its magnetism, soft iron loses it quickly
  Magnetic field lines | Show the direction a north pole would move; closer lines mean a stronger field
    ! Plotting a magnetic field | With a plotting compass or iron filings
  Field of a current | A current in a wire or solenoid has a magnetic field round it
    Strength of the field of a current | Stronger close to the wire and with more current; reversing the current reverses the field
    Relays and loudspeakers | Use the magnetic effect of a current
    Electromagnets | A coil on a soft iron core, switched on and off with the current
  Force on a current | A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force
    Beams of charged particles | A magnetic field pushes a moving charge sideways
    Left-hand rule | Gives the force direction from field and current
    d.c. motor | A coil turns in a field; a split-ring commutator keeps it turning
      Turning effect on a coil | Bigger with more turns, more current or a stronger field
  Electromagnetic induction | A changing magnetic field through a conductor induces an e.m.f.
    Factors affecting induced e.m.f. | Faster movement, a stronger field, more turns
    a.c. generator | A coil rotating in a field gives an alternating e.m.f.
      Slip rings and brushes | Connect the turning coil to the circuit
      Generator output graph | The e.m.f. peaks when the coil cuts the field fastest, zero when the coil is face-on to it
    Direction of induced current | It opposes the change that causes it
  Transformers | Change an alternating voltage using two coils on an iron core
    ! Using IpVp = IsVs | Power in equals power out for a 100% efficient transformer
    ! Power lost in cables | P = I²R, so a smaller current loses less
    ! Using the turns ratio | Vp / Vs = Np / Ns
    High-voltage transmission | Less current for the same power, so less energy lost in the cables

graph: Atoms and radioactivity
Atoms | A tiny positive nucleus with electrons round it
  Ions | Atoms that have lost or gained electrons
  Nuclear model | Alpha scattering showed a small, dense, positive nucleus
    Protons and neutrons | The particles of the nucleus
      Relative charges | Proton +1, neutron 0, electron −1
    Nuclide notation | Proton number and nucleon number written with the symbol
    Isotopes | Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons
  Radioactive decay | Unstable nuclei emit radiation at random
    Why nuclei decay | Too many neutrons, or too heavy; decay makes the nucleus more stable
    Change in beta decay | A neutron becomes a proton and an electron
    Deflection of radiation | Alpha and beta bend opposite ways in electric and magnetic fields; gamma goes straight
    Why alpha ionises most | Its large charge and kinetic energy
    Alpha particles | Two protons and two neutrons: strongly ionising, stopped by paper
    Beta particles | Fast electrons from the nucleus: stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium
    Gamma rays | Electromagnetic waves from the nucleus: weakly ionising, very penetrating
    ! Balancing a decay equation | Proton and nucleon numbers are the same on both sides
    Background radiation | The radiation always around us, from rocks, radon, cosmic rays
      Detecting radiation | A detector, such as a Geiger–Müller tube, connected to a counter
      ! Corrected count rate | Subtract the background count rate
  Half-life | Time for half the nuclei in a sample to decay
    Uses of radioactive isotopes | Smoke alarms, food irradiation, sterilising, thickness gauges, cancer treatment; chosen by radiation type and half-life
    ! Half-life from a decay curve | Read off the time for the count rate to halve
  Fission and fusion | Splitting a heavy nucleus, or joining light ones, releases energy
  Radiation safety | Limit time, keep distance, use shielding
    Effects of radiation on living things | Cell death, mutations and cancer

graph: Space
Space | The Earth, the Solar System and beyond
  Earth and Moon | Rotation gives day and night, the tilt gives seasons, the Moon's orbit its phases
  Solar System | The Sun, eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets
    ! Light travel time | Time = distance / speed of light
    ! Reading planetary data | Compare orbital distance, period, density, surface temperature and field strength
    Orbits | Gravity keeps planets and moons in orbit
      Elliptical orbits | Planets and comets move fastest when nearest the Sun
      Gravitational field of a planet | Stronger for a more massive planet, weaker further away
      Sun's share of the mass | The Sun holds most of the Solar System's mass, so the planets orbit it
    ! Orbital speed | v = 2πr / T for a circular orbit
    Formation of the Solar System | Gravity pulled a cloud of gas and dust into a spinning disc
      Rocky and gas planets | The four inner planets are small and rocky, the four outer ones large and gaseous
  The Sun | A medium-sized star powered by fusing hydrogen into helium
    The Sun's radiation | Mostly infrared, visible and ultraviolet
  Life cycle of stars | From nebula and protostar to a stable star, then giant and dense remnant
    Protostar | A collapsing cloud that heats up; it becomes stable when gravity balances the outward pressure
    Red giants and supergiants | Stars swell when the hydrogen in the core runs low
    White dwarf | What a less massive star leaves after a planetary nebula
    Neutron star | A very dense remnant of a supernova
    Supernova | The explosion of a massive star, making heavy elements
    Black hole | A remnant so dense that light cannot escape
  Galaxies | Collections of billions of stars; ours is the Milky Way
    Milky Way | Our galaxy, about 100 000 light-years across
    Light-year | The distance light travels in a year, 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m
  Expanding Universe | Distant galaxies are moving away from us
    Hubble constant | H₀ = v / d, about 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ per second
    ! Estimating the age of the Universe | d / v = 1 / H₀
    Speed from redshift | The change in wavelength gives a galaxy's speed
    Distance from a supernova | Its brightness gives the distance of its galaxy
    Redshift | Light from receding galaxies is stretched to longer wavelengths
    Big Bang | The Universe began hot and dense about 13.8 billion years ago
    Cosmic microwave background | Leftover radiation from the early Universe
